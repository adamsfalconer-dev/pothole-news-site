Paramount will ask voters on November 3 to tax commercial and industrial property by the square foot, a charge the city says would raise about $4 million a year and that it says no owner of a single-family home, condominium, apartment building or mobile home park, and no residential renter, would owe.

The measure is on the ballot. The Los Angeles County Registrar-Recorder lists it among the measures appearing November 3, under the jurisdiction "City of Paramount," and prints its full question and its passage threshold. The item that put it there went before the City Council at a public hearing on July 28, proposing three resolutions: one calling a special municipal election, one asking the county Board of Supervisors to fold that election into the statewide one the same day, and one approving the measure and ordering it submitted to voters.

## What it charges, and who writes the check

The rates run by property type. Commercial land would pay up to 8 cents per square foot and industrial land up to 11 cents. The highest rate, 13 cents, falls on vacant commercial and industrial land, which is to say on lots with nothing operating on them. The rates phase in over three years, at 65 percent of the full rate in the first year, 80 percent in the second and the full amount from the third on. After that, the city says, they adjust each year with the consumer price index, capped at 3 percent.

The city expects the first bill at the end of 2027, on the property tax bill, at the 65 percent rate.

It estimates that about 80 percent of the owners who would pay owe $5,000 a year or less, and about 90 percent owe $10,000 or less. On the small end, it puts parcels between 228 and 1,178 square feet at $23 to $97 a year. Read the other way, the same estimate leaves about one owner in five expected to owe more than $5,000. The city counts 1,062 business-owned taxable parcels in Paramount: 379 commercial, 579 industrial and 104 vacant. Because the tax is charged by area, the largest parcels would owe the most.

The measure carries no end date. The ballot question says the tax would run "until ended by voters."

## The exemption is the first thing voters will read

The question on the ballot opens with who does not pay. It begins: "Without taxing residential property owners or renters, shall the ordinance to fund services for Paramount 911 response/public safety; reducing crime; responding to business burglaries; addressing street racing; monitoring/improving water/air quality; repairing streets/potholes; supporting businesses by annually levying between 8¢ and 13¢ per square foot on industrial/commercial properties only, with annual adjustments up to 3%, raising approximately $4,000,000 annually until ended by voters, requiring audits, spending disclosure, local control, be adopted?"

Because it is a special tax, dedicated to named purposes rather than to general spending, it needs a two-thirds majority of the ballots cast. The registrar lists the threshold as two-thirds; the city writes it as "a ⅔ supermajority (66.6% + 1)."

That is the shape of the thing. The people who decide it are Paramount's voters. The people who pay it are the owners of the city's commercial and industrial land, who are not necessarily the same people and need not live in Paramount at all.

## What the city says it needs it for

Paramount's case is a budget one. The city projects spending about $49.4 million against about $48.8 million in general fund revenue in the 2026-27 budget year, puts the resulting gap at $545,000, and says that without new money the shortfall accumulates to about $27.7 million over ten years. It says it has already cut operating budgets by 5 percent, frozen vacant positions, trimmed part-time hours and deferred maintenance.

The money this tax raises would not go to that fund. It would go into a dedicated fund, separate from the general fund whose gap the city cites as the reason for the measure, and it could be spent only on four authorized purposes: streets, public safety, air quality monitoring and environmental services, and investment in local businesses. How much goes to each would be set every year in the budget and, the city says, "may fluctuate." The city says the measure would also create an independent citizens' oversight committee, with annual reports posted publicly.

The city names two pressures behind the four purposes. It puts its contract with the Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department at 35 percent of the operating budget, rising 3 to 5 percent a year. And a 2023 citywide study, it says, found $43 million in repairs needed on commercial and industrial streets, $28.7 million of that in a single heavily used industrial area.
