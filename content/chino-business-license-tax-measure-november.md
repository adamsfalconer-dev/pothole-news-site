> *Reported in July 2026 from the City of Chino's own resolution, staff report and election notices, covering the council's July 21 action, as part of our founding archive.*

Chino has capped its business license tax at $1,250 since 1987, no matter how large the business. That single number is what the November 3 ballot measure would remove. The city puts it this way in its staff report: "Removing the cap will ensure that the largest businesses contribute their equitable share without shifting the costs to smaller businesses."

The same report puts numbers on what the cap does now. Chino's top 100 businesses account for 65.29% of all reported gross receipts and pay 12.45% of the business tax the city charges. They employ 6.29% of the workers on the city's license rolls, and the staff report says that figure is part of the mechanism: under Chapter 5.04 of the municipal code, manufacturers, warehouses and administrative headquarters are charged on how many people they employ rather than on what they sell. A warehouse the size of a city block with a small staff is taxed like a small staff. Across about 4,656 active businesses, the tax has brought in between $982,825 and about $1.1 million a year for five years.

Here is what the measure would charge instead. The tax runs on gross receipts, meaning total sales before expenses. Anything with $75,000 or less of them earned in Chino pays a flat $25. Today the minimum is $40 and it applies only up to $25,000, so the floor drops and far more businesses qualify for it: the staff report puts about 56% of Chino businesses at or under $75,000. Above the floor everything is taxed on gross receipts. The headcount method does not appear in the new chapter. Rates run by category, per $1,000 of gross receipts earned in Chino: 60 cents for general and retail, $1 for services and for contractors, and $1.50 for professional firms and for real property and rental activity. The general and retail category is the widest one, and the ordinance writes manufacturers and wholesalers into it, so a manufacturer now assessed on headcount would be charged 60 cents per $1,000 of what it sells. Those are maximums. The ordinance says the top rate applies "unless lowered by appropriate Council action," so a later council can charge less without returning to voters.

At the full rates a shop with $500,000 in Chino sales would owe $300 a year, and a professional practice with the same revenue would owe $750. A business does not reach today's $1,250 ceiling under the new schedule until its receipts pass about $2.1 million in general and retail, about $1.25 million in services and contracting, or about $833,000 for a professional firm. Above those points the measure charges what the cap now blocks. Below them, what a business pays today depends on its category under Chapter 5.04, which is where an owner comparing the two bills has to start. The city projects the tax would bring in about $5.9 million a year in total, roughly $4.8 million more than it collects now.

Here is the whole of what voters will read:

> "Without taxing residents, and to support general city services such as 911/police response; protect local drinking water sources; retain/attract local businesses; shall the measure be adopted to reform City of Chino's outdated business license ordinance with rates of $0.60 - $1.50 per $1,000 of gross receipts for larger businesses based on business category; and only $25 for smaller businesses (under $75,000), generating approximately $4,800,000 annually until ended by voters; with audits, spending disclosure, funds locally controlled?"

State law is specific about what a tax measure's question must contain. Elections Code section 13119 says "the ballot shall include in the statement of the measure to be voted on the amount of money to be raised annually and the rate and duration of the tax to be levied." Chino's question carries all three: the amount, "approximately $4,800,000 annually," which is the new money rather than the $5.9 million total; the rates; and the duration, "until ended by voters," which means there is no sunset.

The cap is not one of the three things the law requires, and the question does not mention it. A voter reading that sentence learns the new rates and has no way to learn from it what rate structure they replace.

Two other things sit in the ordinance rather than in the question. The tax would go to the general fund "available for unrestricted general governmental purposes," which makes it a general tax, passing on a simple majority rather than two thirds, and means the 911 response, the drinking water and the business retention named at the top of the question are what the city says the money is for, not a limit on what it can be spent on. The tax would take effect January 1, 2027, if voters approve it.

The council also appropriated $100,000 for public information about the measure, a one-time allocation on top of the $132,000 the county quoted to place it on the ballot. State law does not prohibit that. Government Code section 54964 bars a local agency from spending funds "to support or oppose" a measure, and then says the section "does not prohibit the expenditure of local agency funds to provide information to the public about the possible effects of a ballot measure on the activities, operations, or policies of the local agency" where two conditions are met, the operative one being that the information "constitutes an accurate, fair, and impartial presentation of relevant facts to aid the voters in reaching an informed judgment regarding the ballot measure." Chino has published a measure page, a fact sheet, a frequently-asked-questions document, the council presentation and a Spanish-language notice, all on the city's own site.

The council voted 5-0 on July 21. Mayor Pro Tem Curtis Burton moved it and Karen C. Comstock seconded; Mayor Eunice M. Ulloa, Christopher Flores and Marc Lucio joined them. The record shows one speaker telling members the ballot language needed refining so an average voter could understand it.
