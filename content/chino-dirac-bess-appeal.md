Chino's city council decides Tuesday night whether to overrule its own planning commission and approve a 400-megawatt battery storage plant near Benson and Oaks avenues. City staff is recommending approval, and a development deal worth at least $10.6 million to the city rides on the answer.

The planning commission turned the project down in July. A motion to approve it failed on a 3-4 vote after residents told commissioners they feared a battery fire, questioned how close the site sits to homes, and pointed to fires at storage facilities elsewhere, according to the city staff report going to the council. The developer, Dirac BESS LLC, appealed on July 24, and under Chino's zoning code the council, not the commission, now has the final word.

For the neighbors who fought the project, Tuesday is the last chance to be heard inside the city's own process: under the zoning code, the council's decision on the appeal is the city's final one. For Chino's budget, the stakes are written into a development agreement the council will consider the same night. Dirac would pay the city $3 million when building permits are issued and another $3 million when the plant opens, plus $600,000 for fire and emergency preparedness. The agreement also routes the project's taxable purchases through Chino and guarantees the city at least $4 million in combined sales tax and development fees, a package staff totals at a minimum of $10.6 million.

The project would put roughly 684 battery enclosures and 86 transformers on 13.89 acres at 13925 Benson Ave. and two Oaks Avenue parcels, replacing three industrial buildings that would be demolished. The batteries would store power from the grid and feed it back at peak demand through a new 230-kilovolt line into the Southern California Edison substation next door. The land and everything surrounding it is zoned general industrial, and the city classifies a battery plant as a major utility allowed there with a special permit.

Fire safety is the argument the appeal has to win. Dirac says the plant would use lithium-iron phosphate batteries housed in separated outdoor enclosures, a design it calls materially different from older facilities such as the Moss Landing plant. The staff report says each enclosure would carry a management system that monitors the batteries around the clock, can shut a failing unit down automatically, and alerts firefighters to the specific unit involved. After the commission's denial, the company asked the city to add conditions to its own permit: continuous carbon monoxide and air monitoring at each enclosure and along the property line, yearly emergency response training for Chino Valley Fire District crews by an outside trainer at Dirac's expense, and a requirement that the fire district sign off in writing before the plant could ever switch battery chemistries. The fire district reviewed the project and attached its own conditions, and an emergency response plan would be required during permitting.

Staff's report carries a warning for the council if it says no. Under a 2022 state law, the report says, Dirac could instead seek its permit from the California Energy Commission, which must decide within 270 days and whose approval replaces the city's permits. If Dirac takes that path, staff writes, the $10.6 million agreement disappears, and any community benefit would be negotiated through the state commission with no guarantee Chino sees a dollar of it.

The fight is not unique to Chino. In June, Covina's planning commission recommended denying a 110-megawatt battery project on Edna Place; that decision also now sits with its city council. In Chino, the timeline after a yes would be short: the agreement commits the city to try to issue demolition permits by Oct. 1, about two weeks after the vote.
