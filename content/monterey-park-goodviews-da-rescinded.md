The Monterey Park City Council finished undoing a five-year-old housing deal on Wednesday, Sept. 2, adopting two ordinances that repeal the custom zoning written in 2021 for 16 gated houses on the hillside at 1688 West Garvey Avenue and terminate the city's development agreement with the company that was going to build them.

The vote was 5-0. Both ordinances rode the consent calendar, the block of items a council passes in a single motion without separate debate, and the minutes record all five council members voting aye. The meeting adjourned at 7:40 p.m. The public hearing and the first reading were two weeks earlier, on Aug. 19. The ordinances take effect in 30 days.

The money at stake is the city's own. Monterey Park spent about $16 million of public funds stabilizing that hillside itself four years ago, after the developer did not, and the council's findings say none of it has been paid back. For the households on the streets below the slope, Wednesday's vote also changed what may someday be built above them.

What the council took back is worth more than what it gave up. The Goodviews Specific Plan, the custom zoning the city wrote for the project in 2021, allows a maximum of 16 single-family houses on the site. The High-Density Residential zoning the council restored on Wednesday allows a maximum of 155 dwelling units. The staff report is direct about why that matters to the city: restoring the higher-density zoning "would allow greater development potential, broaden the potential buyer pool, and maximize the sale price as recommended by the study prepared by Kidder Mathews." The ordinances themselves approve no development.

The two numbers do not settle what can go there. The city's general plan, the document that sits above zoning and says what kind of place each part of the city is meant to be, still designates this parcel Low-Density, which would cap it at 50 units. So the city now has a parcel whose zoning says 155 and whose general plan says 50. A housing project normally has to satisfy both. The staff report notes one way around that: under AB 130, a 2025 state housing law, certain qualifying projects may be treated as consistent if they satisfy either one. In other words, a builder who fits that law could aim at the higher number. If the general plan designation itself were changed to High-Density, the report says, the maximum would be 186 units. In Monterey Park that change would take a vote of the people: the part of the general plan that governs land use can only be amended by voters, a rule the city's own attorney put in writing in March. The record for Wednesday's item does not say whether such a measure will ever be put on a ballot. The housing measure Monterey Park voters will decide on Nov. 3 is a different one, covering the city's commercial and industrial land.

## The $16 million

The reason the city was undoing its own approvals is set out in the ordinances the council adopted, and it is a record of a deal that produced no houses and a very large bill.

The council approved the project on July 7, 2021: a final environmental impact report, a map splitting the land into 18 lots, the zone change creating the Goodviews Specific Plan, and Development Agreement No. 21-01 with Center Int'l Investments, Inc. The agreement required the company to stabilize the hillside. According to the council's findings, it never started. The city manager served a notice of breach on Aug. 25, 2021, and a notice of default on Oct. 13, 2021. In May 2022, the council opened legislative subpoena proceedings, the process a city uses to compel documents and testimony, to gather facts about the nonperformance.

In June 2022 the city declared an emergency and did the hillside work itself, under what it called the Goodviews Abatement Project. Abatement is the term for a government fixing a hazard on private land at the owner's expense. The finding the council adopted on Wednesday states the result plainly: "The City incurred approximately $16 million in costs, secured through nuisance abatement liens. CII has not paid any portion of this amount, which continues to accrue interest at 10 percent annually." A lien is a legal claim recorded against the property, which the city can collect against if the land is ever sold, and which does not require the owner to write a check in the meantime.

The city has recovered part of it in property rather than cash. Under an amended settlement agreement, the company had pledged commercial property on Lee Avenue as security. After it did not reimburse the abatement costs, the city started a foreclosure. The Lee Avenue property drew no buyer at auction on April 2, 2025, title transferred to the city automatically, and the council accepted it on April 16, 2025 as partial satisfaction of the debt. That property, the findings say, is now in escrow for sale.

On May 6 of this year the council held the annual compliance review that the development agreement requires, found the company had not shown good-faith compliance, and directed staff to start unwinding the deal. The findings adopted Wednesday list the specific failures the council relied on. They include the stabilization work, the mitigation measures attached to the environmental report, and the permit applications. They also include the company's refusal to defend and indemnify the city in litigation over the project, captioned Save Our Slopes v. City of Monterey Park. Defend and indemnify is the standard promise a developer makes to pay the city's legal bills and any judgment when someone sues over the developer's project. The council found the company refused, which left those costs with the city.

Center Int'l Investments has not answered any of this on the public record for this item.

## What the city expects to get back

The staff report says the ordinances themselves cost nothing, and that "the anticipated revenue from the sale of the Property under the proposed marketing strategy, is expected to offset a significant portion of the outstanding abatement costs." It does not say what the property is expected to fetch, and the Kidder Mathews study is not attached to the packet.

There is also something the record for this item does not explain. The foreclosure and the transfer of title it describes are the Lee Avenue property, not the hillside. How the city came to control the sale of 1688 West Garvey Avenue is not stated anywhere in Wednesday's packet. That question, and what the marketing strategy actually proposes, are the next things worth reading.

**What's next:**

The ordinances take effect 30 days after adoption, which falls in early October. The council's next regular meetings are the ones to watch for a disposition or marketing item on the property. Monterey Park posts its agendas and its action minutes at www.montereypark.ca.gov/agendas.
