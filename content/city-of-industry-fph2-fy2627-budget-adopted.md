The public hydrogen agency that City of Industry helped create adopted its 2026-27 budget on July 16, and the single largest thing that budget counts on is money from selling hydrogen: $6,869,760 in sales, or $1,941,065 after the cost of the fuel itself. That net figure is 41 percent of the $4,716,065 the agency forecast it would have coming in this year.

One month into the fiscal year, on July 31, it had sold none of it.

The First Public Hydrogen Authority is a joint powers agency, a government the cities of Industry and Lancaster formed together in December 2024. Its board met in Industry's council chambers on July 16 and, on a motion by Vice Chair Ruggles seconded by Board Member Peacock, voted unanimously to adopt the fiscal 2026-27 budget and rescind the stopgap resolution that had been keeping the lights on without one. Board Member Medrano was absent. That account comes from the meeting's summary minutes, which are still unapproved and which went onto the board's Aug. 20 consent calendar alongside the first treasurer's report written under the new budget.

The treasurer's report, which is unaudited, puts a number on the plan. As of July 31, the authority's comparison of budget against actual results lists net hydrogen sales of $0 against the budgeted $1,941,065, leaving 100 percent of the line untouched. Everything it did earn, $300,673, came from professional services it provided to others, $300,000 of it, plus $673 in interest; the report does not say who was billed. Its balance sheet also carries a $300,000 account receivable, which is money the authority is owed and has not yet collected. It recorded $319,158 in expenses over the same period, which the treasurer's summary puts at "9.8% of FPH2 total annual adopted budget" against a $3,256,445 spending plan.

A month of no hydrogen sales is not by itself a broken promise. The budget's hydrogen figures were written, in the staff report's words, to "reflect further refinement on when sales activity is projected to begin." What the record does not contain is a buyer, or a firm date. The same staff report, dated July 16 and written for the year that had just started, says staff "continues to work diligently to finalize agreements with potential offtakers for supply to be delivered early in the next fiscal year." An offtaker is the trade's word for the customer at the far end of a supply contract. Neither the July packet nor the August one names one.

The debt behind all of it is one city's. The authority's assets on July 31 totaled $633,060, of which $262,451 was cash. Its liabilities totaled $6,219,227, of which $5,836,212 is owed to the City of Lancaster, which lent the authority its startup money. That leaves a net position, the accounting term for what is left when liabilities are subtracted from assets, of negative $5,586,167.

That number is less alarming than it looks. A brand new joint powers agency financed entirely by a member city's loan carries a negative net position by construction: the loan is a liability from the day it is drawn, and the money it paid for is spent. The figure is not a verdict on solvency. What it does measure is how much has been borrowed and not yet earned back.

The borrowing has a ceiling. Lancaster approved a promissory note of $6,600,000; $5,600,000 of principal has been drawn and $236,212 of interest has accrued on it, which is the $5,836,212 on the balance sheet, and interest keeps accruing monthly at a current rate of 3.840 percent. That leaves $1,000,000 of principal available, and it is the only borrowing on the authority's balance sheet.

The month itself was close to a wash: $300,673 in revenue against $319,158 in recorded expenses, for an operating loss of $18,485. The authority is not, on this month's evidence, burning through what remains. It is also not earning any of it back.

The board raised the repayment question itself. According to the minutes, at the meeting where the budget was adopted, Board Member Brouwer asked whether services being bought under professional services agreements could be brought in house, when additional hiring was expected, and when the loan from Lancaster is due to be repaid, and Board Member Peacock asked about the status of the open purchase orders. The minutes record no answer to any of the four, and then record the motion and the unanimous vote. These are summary minutes rather than a transcript, so what they show is that no answer was written down, not that none was given.

What Industry has at stake in this is a different question from what Lancaster does, and the records answer it plainly: the money is Lancaster's. Industry co-founded the authority and holds a seat on its board, and its council chambers are where the board meets. No debt to Industry appears anywhere on the authority's balance sheet.
