The monthly charge Glendale bills a home with the smallest water meter, before a drop is used, would jump from $19.37 to $42 on Jan. 1, a 117 percent increase, under the draft rate plan in front of the City Council tonight.

Tonight's vote does not adopt the rates. The council decides at 6 p.m. whether to mail the legal notice that starts the clock under Proposition 218, the state constitutional rule that lets the people who pay for a service block a rate increase by majority protest. The draft notice sets the public hearing for 6 p.m. on Tuesday, Nov. 3, at City Hall, 613 E. Broadway.

The increases run five years. The basic charge on a 5/8-inch meter, the smallest size in the schedule, would step up each Jan. 1 and reach $60.36 in 2031, just over three times today's $19.37. The charge for water actually used rises too. A single-family home now pays $2.80 per unit for its first 8 units each month, with a unit, called an HCF, equal to about 748 gallons. The plan moves every customer class to two price tiers. For a home on the small meters, that means $4.09 per unit for the first 10 units and $6.92 above that in 2027, climbing to $5.87 and $9.95 by 2031.

Put together, a house on the smallest meter using 8 units a month pays $41.77 today and would pay $74.72 under the 2027 rates before the new pump-zone charge, a 79 percent increase. A low-use household at 3 units goes from $27.77 to $54.27 before that charge, up 95 percent, because the fixed charge more than doubles no matter how little water flows. Owners of 1-inch meters would see their basic charge dip 37 cents in 2027 before it rises with everyone else's in 2028.

Different customers feel it differently, and some of the plan is new structure, not just new prices. That pump-zone charge is new for everyone: every address would pay it on every unit, from 5 cents in the lowest of seven elevation zones to $5.08 in the highest in 2027, and the higher the water has to be pumped, the more it costs. Apartment buildings, which now get their first-tier allotment per dwelling unit, would instead get one set by meter size. Businesses would move from a flat $4.04 per unit to a two-tier schedule of their own, $4.17 and $6.92 to start. Separate schedules for recycled water and private fire lines change as well.

The notice attributes the increases to the cost of imported water and to a system it says has aged beyond its useful life. By the notice's own accounting, Glendale Water & Power buys 60 to 70 percent of its supply from the Metropolitan Water District, whose rates have climbed about 34 percent since July 2022, and the city hired a rate consultant, Raftelis, to run the cost-of-service study behind the numbers.

A ratepayer who wants a say has three windows. Tonight's meeting takes public comment in person and online. Four community meetings follow on Oct. 7, 15, 17 and 24, at venues including the Adult Recreation Center, the Sparr Heights Community Center, the Brand Park Library and one session on Zoom; the notice points readers to GlendaleCA.gov/ProposedWaterRates for the details. The only objection that counts toward stopping the rates, though, is a written protest on paper: signed, one per parcel, identifying the property or account, and received before the hearing ends on Nov. 3, which is also Election Day. Emails, faxes and spoken objections are not tallied. If a majority of the parcels served, half plus one, file protests, the city cannot adopt the increases. Anything short of that and the council votes after the hearing, with power to trim the numbers but not raise them beyond what the notice states.

If the rates pass, they take effect Jan. 1, with a further increase each Jan. 1 through 2031.
