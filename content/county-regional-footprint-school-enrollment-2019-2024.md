> *Reported from federal enrollment files covering the 2019-20 through 2024-25 school years, published as part of our founding archive.*

Thirty-one public school districts serve the cities between Pasadena and Chino Hills. They enrolled 334,126 children in 2019-20 and 298,984 in 2024-25, the most recent year the federal government has published. That is 35,142 fewer children, a 10.5% drop, and more students than Chino Valley Unified, the largest district in the footprint, has altogether.

What happens to a district's money when children leave is set out in a single sentence of state law, and it is the reason the consequences have not arrived yet.

California does not pay districts for the children on their rolls. It pays for average daily attendance, the count of pupils actually in seats on an average day, which always runs below enrollment. Education Code section 42238.05 then tells a district which year's attendance to use: the current year, "or the prior fiscal year if the prior fiscal year total is greater, or the average of the three most recent prior fiscal years, if the three-year average total is greater than both." A district losing students is therefore funded on its best recent year rather than on the children in its classrooms. The money lags the enrollment by up to three years.

That lag is a cushion, and it is the reason to pay attention rather than a reason not to. It lets a district keep staff it can no longer fund from current attendance. It also expires, and when it does, the reduction is measured against the accumulated loss rather than a single year of it.

Hacienda La Puente Unified is what that looks like written down, in its own budget. The district says it uses the three-prior-year average, and describes it as a method that "moderates the immediate fiscal impact of single-year declines but does not eliminate them." Its own table prices the cushion for this school year: the state funds the district on 14,799 students of attendance while the district expects 14,282 to show up. It is paid for 517 children it does not have.

That is the cushion working, not running out, and the district is planning around it anyway. The same budget, $351.2 million adopted in June, plans a reduction of 25 certificated positions, meaning teachers and other credentialed staff, and the budget ties that cut to savings "aligned with declining enrollment." It also plans about $15 million of spending beyond what it takes in this year, and projects enrollment falling from 15,184 students in 2025-26 to 14,123 by 2028-29. A three-year average that is dragged down every year by a smaller new year does not deliver one cliff. It delivers a smaller number every year, which is why the staffing plan starts before the money runs out.

Pomona Unified is what it looks like on a street. The district, which serves parts of Diamond Bar as well as Pomona, filed notice in May that it is closing Armstrong Elementary School in Diamond Bar and moving all 215 of its students to Golden Springs Elementary, also in Diamond Bar, which enrolls 388 and has room for 870. The district's stated reason, in the filing: to "address declining enrollment and ensure the efficient allocation of resources." The filing puts the consolidation in the summer of 2026, in effect for the 2026-27 school year. Those 215 children start the year at Golden Springs.

The two districts named Mountain View Elementary are not the same district. One is in El Monte and lost more than a quarter of its students. One is in Ontario and gained nearly a third. The Ontario district grew in every one of the five years, 2,540 then 2,625, 2,707, 3,035, 3,156, 3,318, and opened a fifth school along the way.

One number in the table is already out of date in the direction that matters. Hacienda La Puente's own budget puts it at 15,184 students for this school year, below the 15,480 the federal file reports for 2024-25. Either the slide has continued past the table's last column, or the two counts are measuring slightly different things. The records support both readings and settle neither.

What is driving the decline is not established here. These are the federal government's figures, from the annual census every public school district files with the National Center for Education Statistics; California's Department of Education collects its own counts under its own rules, and where a district's own budget states a number, the budget is the better source.
