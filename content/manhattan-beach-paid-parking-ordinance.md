Manhattan Beach's more than 1,275 single-space parking meters are scheduled under the city's rollout plan to disappear this November, replaced by about 140 pay stations plus app and text-message payments, and on Sept. 15 the City Council voted 5-0 to introduce the ordinance that rewrites the city's parking rules to match.

The vote was a first reading, so nothing changes yet. Ordinance No. 26-0011 needs a second vote to become law. The staff report prepared for the city's parking commission penciled that in for Oct. 6, and city staff wrote that the rules would take effect in November, timed to the arrival of the new equipment.

For anyone who parks in the city, whether a resident on a metered block, a beachgoer, or a downtown shopper, the changes come in three layers.

Payment moves off the meter. Drivers will pay at a pay station, some card-only and some taking card and coin, or by app or text message, and, city staff says, will be able to add time from a phone up to the posted maximum without walking back to the car. The council approved a three-year agreement with ParkMobile in February to supply the stations and the mobile system. One thing the city says it will not do is use QR codes anywhere in the payment process, an approach staff says is intended to head off security concerns.

Enforcement gets automated. Under an agreement approved in May, parking enforcement vehicles will carry cameras that read license plates and check them against paid sessions, using time-stamped plate images instead of marking tires, according to the staff report.

And the rulebook changes. The ordinance adds a new Municipal Code chapter, 14.42, that lets the council set rates, enforcement days and hours, and time limits later by resolution, which staff says allows parking rules that vary by date, day of the week, season or location. The chapter also writes some new tickets into the code. Re-parking in the same paid zone to get around a time limit becomes its own violation: once a car hits the posted maximum, parking anywhere in that zone during a council-set "time-out" period is ticketable, and each re-park counts separately. A driver cited for not paying who stays past the zone's limit keeps accruing violations. And overstaying a posted time limit is a violation whether or not the space requires payment.

None of this sets what parking will actually cost. Rates come later, by council resolution, and paid parking in any zone kicks in only once the machines are installed there.

The transition is not free. Staff estimated implementation at about $3.09 million, including pay stations, signage, digital displays and license plate reader upgrades, with about $475,300 a year in ongoing costs for software licensing, transaction fees and warranty coverage.

One piece of the old system survives: the city's long-established merchant parking permit program stays, and the old parking meter chapter of the code is being renamed Merchant Parking Permits to hold it.
