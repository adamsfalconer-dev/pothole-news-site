A La Cañada Flintridge resident who cannot get to City Hall on a Tuesday evening can now speak at a City Council meeting from a phone. Since July 21 every council agenda has printed the number, 1-669-444-9171, along with a Zoom link and that meeting's ID. A caller gets the same three minutes the agenda gives anyone standing at the podium, because the law requires the same time allotment either way.

People have used it. The minutes record a virtual speaker at the July 21 special meeting, two at the July 21 regular meeting and one at the Aug. 4 meeting. Among them were David Haxon, who called in twice, and Planning Commissioner Nerses Aposhian.

The option exists because of Senate Bill 707, which added a remote-access requirement to the state's open-meetings law. That requirement took effect July 1. It reaches La Cañada Flintridge through Los Angeles County: the law covers any city council in a county of 600,000 or more people, whatever the size of the city itself, and the city's own attorney reached that conclusion in writing. The requirement runs through 2029 and is repealed on Jan. 1, 2030.

Getting there took the council two tries. The minutes of the June 16 meeting record the policy implementing the law as item 32 on the consent calendar, one of 44 items passed together on a single 5-0 roll call. Council members had questions about it, along with three other items, and voted it through on consent anyway.

The law does not allow that. The statute says the policy covering what happens when the remote connection fails must be approved "at a noticed public meeting in open session, not on the consent calendar." City Attorney Adrian Guerra put the problem in a staff report five weeks later: the policy, he wrote, "was inadvertently adopted under the Consent Calendar as Resolution No. 26-25." He brought it back on July 21 as its own agenda item, and the council readopted it 5-0 at a special meeting that day, three weeks past the state's deadline.

Nobody lost a turn to the delay. The council's July 7 meeting was canceled, so July 21 was its first meeting after the deadline anyway, and Mayor Kim Bowman opened both of the council's July 21 meetings by announcing that the hybrid format started that day.

The agendas also tell a caller what happens if the connection drops. Under a heading reading "Technical Disruptions," all five council agendas since July 21 say the council will recess if a technical problem stops the public from watching or taking part remotely and city staff cannot correct it immediately. If remote access is not back within an hour, the agendas say, the council may continue only after confirming that the city made good faith efforts and finding that the public interest in proceeding outweighs the need for remote access. The law sets the outer bounds: the recess runs at least an hour unless service comes back sooner, and a council that reconvenes without remote access has to adopt that finding by roll call vote. No such failure appears in the minutes of the council's July and August meetings.

The option stops at the council's own dais. The policy covers a commission only if the council authorizes that commission by resolution, and on Aug. 4 the council balked at doing that all at once, citing costs, staffing needs and technical complexity. By consensus, with no vote taken, it told staff to come back with a phased pilot. Staff did, on Sept. 1: a recommendation to authorize hybrid meetings for the Planning Commission and the Public Works and Traffic Commission, and to move $2,850 out of the general fund to pay six months of audiovisual support for the latter. What the council did with that recommendation is not yet on the public record.

The Planning Commission and the council meet in the same room. Their minutes give the same address and the same chamber. The city's own staff report says the audiovisual staff who would run a remote hookup are already on site for planning nights. Someone with a rate question for the council can call in. Someone whose neighbor has applied to build next door still has to show up, because the Planning Commission is where that gets decided. Its agenda for Thursday offers a live stream to watch and no way to speak.
