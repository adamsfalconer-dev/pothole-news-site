> *Compiled in July 2026 from State Controller filings for the 2023-24 fiscal year, the most recent published, as part of our founding archive.*

Diamond Bar spends $735 a year for each of its residents. Ontario spends $3,401. Every other city in this footprint sits between them except one, and most fall between $1,000 and $1,750.

That figure is the simplest useful thing a resident can know about a city budget: everything the city spent, divided by everyone who lives there. Below is where all nineteen land, from the state's own filings for the 2023-24 fiscal year, the most recent published.

Two entries need a word before the rest makes sense.

Irwindale is its own category and belongs in the table anyway. It has 1,499 residents and an industrial tax base built for a city many times that size, so its $38.8 million in spending works out to $25,911 per resident. Take the other eighteen cities together, and they spend $1,714 for each of the 1.28 million people who live in them. Irwindale spends about fifteen times that. It is a real number and it is not a comparable one.

Ontario is the largest city here, with 181,224 residents to Pomona's 152,166, and it is also the highest per-resident spender of the eighteen. Those are two separate facts: dividing by population removes size from the figure, so being big is not what puts a city high on this list. Ontario also spent the most beyond what it took in, $616.3 million against $543.3 million in revenue.

Four of the nineteen cities spent more than they collected. Ontario's gap was $73 million and Montebello's was $36.8 million, and Montebello, about a third Ontario's size, ran the deeper gap per person at $595 for every resident. Pomona was short $8.6 million and El Monte $4 million.

Two things have to be said about those four.

One year is not a trend. These are the 2023-24 figures, the most recent the Controller has published, and two budget cycles have been adopted since. One year cannot show direction.

A deficit here is not insolvency. A city that finishes a year spending more than it took in may be doing exactly what it planned: drawing down a reserve for a capital project, closing out a bond-funded build, absorbing a one-time cost. The state's file records the totals and not the reasons. Telling a deliberate drawdown from a structural gap takes the city's own annual financial report, and neither Ontario's nor Montebello's was read for this account.

The figures are also broader than the general fund residents hear about at a council meeting. They include capital-project and special-revenue money, so a city's general fund can be balanced while this number is not.

And the number counts what a city spends through its own budget, which is not the same list of things in every city. Two cities can spend very differently per resident and hand a resident much the same services, or spend alike and hand them different ones. A low figure is not by itself a lean city and a high one is not by itself a generous one.

Three places here have no row. Rowland Heights and Hacienda Heights are unincorporated, so Los Angeles County reports for them and the county's figure cannot be split by community. City of Industry has no row in the state's file under that name, which is a gap in the data rather than a finding about the city.
