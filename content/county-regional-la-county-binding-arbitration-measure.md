A charter amendment on the November 3 ballot would take the last word on what Los Angeles County pays the workers in 14 public-safety bargaining units, from sheriff's deputies to lifeguards, away from the five elected supervisors and hand it to three arbitrators. It is on the ballot because the county's charter is its founding document, and only voters can change it.

Today, when the county and one of those unions cannot agree on a new contract, the two sides go through mediation and fact-finding, which ends in a report. The Board of Supervisors then holds a hearing and can put its own last, best and final offer into effect over the union's objection. Those workers cannot lawfully strike.

The ordinance does not hedge: "No other actions by the Board of Supervisors or by the electorate to confirm or approve the decision of the Board of Arbitrators shall be permitted or required."

Under the measure, today's mediation and fact-finding rules would no longer apply to these units. A deadlock over a successor contract, the agreement that replaces one about to expire, would go to a three-member panel: one arbitrator named by each side, plus a neutral chair. The panel does not split the difference. It votes issue by issue, adopting one side's final offer on each, against criteria that include "the financial condition and obligations of the County and its ability to meet the multi-year ongoing costs of the decision." Then it binds, and the two sides split the cost of getting there.

The ordinance lists 14 bargaining units by number. The ballot question spells out who is in them, and the list runs well past deputies and firefighters: lifeguards, district attorney investigators, deputy medical examiners and civilian staff in those departments. Strikes stay prohibited, and willfully joining one remains grounds for discipline "up to and including termination."

The case for it starts with that strike ban. Third District Supervisor Lindsey P. Horvath's February 10 motion, which asked County Counsel to write the measure, describes where today's process ends: the board "holds a public hearing and can implement its last, best, and final offer terms and conditions over the objections of the union." That, the motion says, is "challenging for public safety employees," who under state law cannot walk out.

In a February 9 memo, Acting Chief Executive Officer Joseph M. Nicchitta told the supervisors: "Earlier this year, union representatives notified me that they had begun the signature gathering process to place a measure on the November 2026 ballot." The version in the motion before the board, he said, covers fewer units and fewer employees than the union's and reaches only successor contracts. On the county's own account, the board was not choosing between arbitration and none. It was writing a narrower version of a measure already gathering signatures. What became of the union's own drive, the records do not say; the registrar's list of county measures, dated July 24, carries one, and it is this one.

Nicchitta argued against the board's version anyway. Arbitrators' decisions "will be binding on your Board, even if your Board disagrees with the decisions," he wrote, even where a decision requires the board "to redirect funding from your policy priorities or safety net services." He also wrote that voter-approved mandatory arbitration "has caused significant financial disruption and even prompted bankruptcy for cities in California and other parts of the country," a claim he backed with two attached newspaper articles, not county analysis. He asked for nine safeguards.

The board adopted the ordinance 4-1 on March 17. Supervisors Hilda L. Solis, Lindsey P. Horvath, Janice Hahn and Kathryn Barger voted yes; Second District Supervisor Holly J. Mitchell was the lone no. Two of the nine safeguards were picked up in part: the certified ordinance requires mediation before arbitration and widens what the panel must weigh about county finances. The other seven, among them a delayed start in 2028, a sunset date and an express right to challenge an award in court, do not appear in it.

The largest dollar figure in the board's package is Nicchitta's: "In Fiscal Year 2025-26, the County has budgeted $20.8 billion for salaries and employee benefits." That is every county employee, not the 14 units this measure reaches. Nothing in the package breaks out what those units cost, and nothing in it says how many people work in them.

A price is coming, because of something the board did two years ago. On July 23, 2024, the supervisors voted 5-0 to instruct the Auditor-Controller to cost out every countywide ballot measure, with an amendment from Supervisor Janice Hahn sending the statement to the board before it reaches the registrar. The registrar's calendar prints the requirement as standing policy: "Fiscal impact statements are required for any election that includes a countywide measure affecting County revenues or expenditures."

What the documents do not agree on is when. The 2024 motion sets the work "not later than 88 days before" such an election, which for November 3 is August 7. The board's own record of that meeting prints the direction without the 88-day clause, and the registrar's calendar recommends August 14 for transmitting the finished statement. Elections Code section 9160 sets the auditor no deadline at all. Nothing in the record says which date governs.

The board's resolution orders the full ordinance printed in the sample ballot. Right now a voter has the ordinance and the vote count. Everything written to help them weigh it is due next month.
