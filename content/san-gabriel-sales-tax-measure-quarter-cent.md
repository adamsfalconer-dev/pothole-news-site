San Gabriel shoppers are being asked in November to add a penny to every dollar they spend in the city. The city's own report to the council says that under current state law it can collect a quarter of it.

The question voters will see, as filed with the Los Angeles County registrar, asks whether "a measure establishing a 1¢ sales tax providing approximately $7,100,000 annually until ended by voters, requiring audits, spending disclosure, funds locally controlled" should be adopted. It runs on the ballot as San Gabriel Public Safety/Local Control. Before that ask, the question runs through a list of purposes: the city's financial stability, medical 911 and police response, fire services, crime prevention, drinking water sources, streets and potholes, clean and safe public areas, safe schools, earthquake and disaster response, keeping and attracting firefighters and, last, "other general services." The measure is a general tax. The resolution placing it calls it "a new general tax," and the ordinance says the revenue "may be used for any unrestricted general revenue purpose."

The staff report in the packet for the council's July 21 meeting, where the measure was placed, puts two numbers on the tax. Its fiscal impact section says it "would generate approximately $7.0 million annually." Its narrative says the city cannot collect that yet. "Under current state law, San Gabriel voters have the ability to increase the local TUT by one-quarter cent, which would generate approximately $1.7 million annually," it says, using the city's shorthand for a transactions and use tax. Any amount above a quarter cent "would be contingent upon state legislation enabling San Gabriel to exceed the cap," the report continues. "The one-quarter cent allowed under the current cap space would be collected until the cap is increased and the additional amount would be collected from that point forward."

The ballot names about four times what the city says it can currently take in: a gap of $5.4 million a year against the ballot's figure, $5.3 million against the $7.0 million the report itself uses. On a $100 purchase, it is the difference between a dollar and 25 cents.

The ordinance voters would enact spells none of that out. It sets one rate, "one percent (1.0%) of the gross receipts of any retailer," and lists four conditions for taking effect: a two-thirds council vote, approval by a majority of voters, certification of the results and the council's confirmation afterward. State legislation is not among them, and the words "cap," "legislation" and "quarter cent" appear nowhere in its nine pages. The ordinance does bind itself to the state's transactions-and-use-tax law. It imposes the tax "in accordance with" that law and accepts "the requirements and limitations" in it, which is where the ceiling lives.

If the cap does not move, this is a tax that takes San Gabriel from 10.5% to 10.75% and raises $1.7 million a year, not the $7.1 million on the ballot.
